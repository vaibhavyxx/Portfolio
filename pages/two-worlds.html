Coming up with this concept was hard, I never had my Eureka moment but I rather found my pleasure in the process. So, when I first chose code poetry, I had no idea how to go into it. I read a lot of the poems from Code-poetry (n.d.) to understand how they are composed. I have to admit writing a poem in coding syntax was a lot easier than coming up with its visual element. I never thought that I would share this work of mine with anyone as I wrote for myself as a way to describe my feelings of living far from home. Hence, my poem’s central theme is identity.

For the poem, I chose Python as anyone can understand it. (It’s true because I had made my Photography professor read my work for feedback and she didn’t have issues with understanding it.) Through my peer’s feedback, I was encouraged to be more creative with the use of functions, dictionaries, variables, etc. to communicate my ideas instead of using comments. Croll’s work on adapting different authorial voices to show how they would code was my biggest source of inspiration for the poem (2014).

Now, as of the visual element, well.. It was harder than I imagined as I found it vague. At one point, I had considered blending my poetry and visual element together as the user would have to interact with it to be able to see the next stanza. However, it was a very rudimentary feature and my professor suggested that I should “probably add a bit more pizazz ” (personal communication, October 7, 2024). So, I decided to make a website instead with two groups of circles moving in the opposite direction and when they would intersect, they would change colors. If I am honest, I did not come up with this idea until I was scrolling through a Flocking example (Flocking, n.d.) Since I have coded Non Playable Character movement before, I wanted to take this as an opportunity to strengthen my concept and understand the math behind it. Hence, I followed Shiffman’s tutorial on Flocking Simulation (2018) as I had a tight deadline to finish the project. When I was almost done with the project, I decided to goof around and see how I optimize my code better. By simply not clearing out the screen after every frame, I made my work more appealing and stand out.

So, this is how I ended up making Two Worlds. With three sliders - Community, Conflict, and Flow for users to change the brush strokes movement, I wanted to show how our experiences in life can shape us. These strokes were a metaphor for memories and as blank canvas as to how much we can remember so after a while, it is difficult to remember who you used to be. Adjusting to a new culture is hard especially when you are balancing your old one and this project is a great visual aid for it. After all, who are you after all these changes?
